# Track-note prose guide (2026-10-06)

Why this exists: Wesley read the Week 2 song listings and called them "LLM prosey."
Deep research into human-written listening prose (11 sources, verbatim quotes)
identified the failure mode and the fix. Every future digest's track notes must
follow this. Source report: `~/workspace/research_notes/music-listening-prose-models-20261006-0400/report.md`.

## The failure mode

The old notes all shared one template: "**[Element] as [poetic noun]:** [sentence].
The pocket/groove [verb]..." — 78 Mad Libs with different adjectives. Any note
could describe any song. That interchangeability is the LLM tell.

## Principles (with human models)

1. **Bold lead = the vocabulary word only.** `**Panning:**`, `**Timbre:**` — never
   "**X as Y**" poetry ("Texture as water," "Dynamics as tide"). The word teaches;
   the simile slot is where the LLM hides.
2. **Christgau compression.** Name concrete sounds, set them against each other.
   "xylophonish tinkle countered by a keyb belch like an engine that won't catch."
   — Robert Christgau, Consumer Guide
   (https://www.robertchristgau.com/get_album.php?id=4045).
   Eight words, could only be that record. This is the target density.
3. **Ross cause → effect.** Name the event, then its consequence, one sentence.
   "When the harmony sinks from D major to D minor, the melody takes on a more
   jagged, unsettled character." — Alex Ross, New Yorker (via
   https://slippedisc.com/2023/05/alex-ross-deconstructs-dudamel/).
4. **Padgham correction.** Correct the likely mishearing, then name the mechanism.
   "It is not really that live, it's big... made from a lot of compression with
   ambient mics as far away from the drums as possible, and those are noise-gated."
   — Hugh Padgham on "In the Air Tonight," Mix magazine
   (https://www.mixonline.com/recording/classic-tracks-phil-collins-air-tonight-365521).
5. **Tate function.** Give an ugly/strange sound its musical job.
   "Feedback... became for Hendrix a means of expanding the instrument's
   sustaining capacity, as a violinist does with her bow." — Greg Tate,
   *Midnight Lightning* (via https://www.khsu.org/2021-12-11/stonecoldboldness-a-many-sided-memorial-to-the-writing-of-greg-tate).
6. **Perry physical chain.** Action → material source → pinned to the track.
   "By turning his amp up high and treading the pedal he found he could modulate
   the natural hiss of amplifier valves." — John Perry, 33⅓ *Electric Ladyland*
   (https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/45869326-electric-ladyland).
7. **Wright one-element rule.** One element at one timestamp; refuse the
   whole-impression sentence. "The first I couldn't pick out any melody at all.
   It was all what? Rhythm and beat." — Craig Wright, Yale MUSI 112
   (https://oyc.yale.edu/music/musi-112/lecture-1).
8. **Eno's metaphor with a job.** Metaphors are allowed only attached to a named
   device/action: "piddling around with the sound until I make it sound like a
   drop of water falling into a pool." — Brian Eno (via
   https://www.musicradar.com/news/classic-brian-eno-interview-reveals-how-he-really-produces-ambient-music).
9. **Vary the sentence shapes.** Across a week's notes, rotate: correction
   (Padgham), inventory-then-verdict (Christgau: "the interplay of amped-up
   bass, heartbeat drums, and scratch guitar"), cause→effect (Ross), physical
   chain (Perry), bright-line reframe (MacDonald: "Beyond the basic words and
   music lay the vital work of arranging"). Never the same opening twice in a row.
10. **The week's concept must be earned per note.** Don't staple "the pocket"
    or "the groove" into every sentence. Use it only where the note actually
    demonstrates it; otherwise the concept lives in the Listen-For line.

## Anti-patterns (each with its fix)

1. Generic adjectives ("haunting," "ethereal") → swap for the physical cause
   (long reverb tail, high register, soft attack).
2. Unearned metaphors ("a cathedral of sound") → attach to a named instrument,
   action, or device.
3. Vibe-only verdicts ("this track slaps") → describe what changes between two
   timestamps instead.
4. Passive production claims ("drenched in reverb") → who did what with what.
5. Emotional projection ("the guitar cries out") → transcribe what the hands do;
   let the feeling follow.
6. One-whole-song blur → one element, one timestamp (Wright's rule).
7. Cliché period labels ("very '80s") → name the actual sound (gated snare,
   DX7 bell).

## Standing constraints (unchanged)

- 2–3 lines per note, direct and dry, verbs over adjectives.
- Opinion/cue only: describe what is audible. No unverifiable studio lore; if a
  note asserts a production fact, it needs a source or it gets cut.
- Timestamps and track titles come from the tracklist cache; never invent them.
