ANCHOR
KING CRIMSON 1969

In the Court of the Crimson King

The record that invented a genre before the genre had a name. King Crimson cut their debut at Wessex Studios in the summer of 1969 — five players, an 8-track machine, and Ian McDonald’s mellotron stacked five tape generations deep until it sounded like an orchestra. ’21st Century Schizoid Man’ opens with a distorted guitar-and-sax unison riff that still sounds violent; ‘Epitaph’ answers with the most mournful keyboard part in rock. This is your anchor because everything else this week is measured against it: while Barrett was gone from Floyd, London’s underground was building this. Scores, for the press-play question: BestEverAlbums 88.8/100 (Bayesian average, top 1% of all albums on the site); Rolling Stone’s #2 prog album of all time (2015); Pitchfork 10/10. At release, Robert Christgau called it ‘ersatz shit’. The canon disagreed.

LISTEN FOR — Track the mellotron across the record — on ‘Epitaph’ it carries two parts at once, volumes shifting against each other so the sustained chords seem to breathe; on ‘21st Century Schizoid Man’ the same keyboard turns into a weapon.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. 21st Century Schizoid Man0:00
    Distortion: The opening riff is guitar and saxophone playing the same line, both distorted — the sax sounds like it’s running through a guitar amp because it practically is. Nobody else in 1969 paired those two instruments and turned them both up.
  2. 2. I Talk to the Wind7:22
    Texture: After the opener’s violence, flute and brushed drums enter like a different band took the stage. The softness only lands because of what came first — sequencing as dynamics.
  3. 3. Epitaph13:28
    Voicing: McDonald recorded two separate mellotron parts and rode their volumes against each other in the mix — the trick that gives the sustained chords a sense of motion instead of sitting flat. It’s audible: listen to the chords swell and recede.
  4. 4. Moonchild22:15
    Form: The song ends around the three-minute mark; what follows is nine minutes of cymbal-and-guitar improvisation. The form dissolves on purpose — the record’s strangest gamble, and its quietest stretch.
  5. 5. The Court of the Crimson King34:27
    Arrangement: Three verses separated by instrumental fanfares, each bigger than the last — a pop structure inflated to cathedral scale. The mellotron doesn’t decorate the song; it is the orchestra.
  6. 6. 21st Century Schizoid Man (Radio Version)44:30
    Density: The single edit: same violence, half the room. Compressing seven minutes into single length shows what the verses were holding up — the edit keeps the riff and loses the architecture.
  7. 7. I Talk to the Wind (Duo Version)51:11
    Space: Two players, no band — the song survives on flute and voice alone. With the drums and bass gone, the melody has to carry everything, and does.
  8. 8. A Man A City (Live at the Fillmore West)55:57
    Energy: One continuous live take from the band’s early touring — no overdubs, no second chances. The studio album is precise; this is the same band breathing in real time.

ADVENTUROUS
PINK FLOYD 1977

Animals

The immediate hangover after Wish You Were Here — and the darkest record Pink Floyd ever made. Recorded through 1976 at Britannia Row, the band’s own studio, Animals rebuilds two leftover live songs into ‘Sheep’ and ‘Dogs’ and frames them with Roger Waters’ loose rewrite of Orwell’s Animal Farm: dogs as predators, pigs as despots, sheep as the herd. Gilmour plays like a man with something to prove — ‘Dogs’ runs seventeen minutes on his longest, most lyrical solos, smeared through the Binson Echorec echo unit, the same model Syd Barrett had used. Scores: BestEverAlbums 88/100 (2,821 votes); Pitchfork 10/10. Rolling Stone was unimpressed in 1977; the decades reversed the verdict. This week it’s the direct line from your island album #1 — same band, two years later, no warmth left.

LISTEN FOR — Gilmour’s guitar through the Binson — on ‘Dogs’ the echo repeats smear each note into the next, so the vast space is part of the riff, not decoration. This is the week’s technique: listen for how the repeats do work that reverb alone couldn’t.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Pigs on the Wing 10:00
    Form: Eighty-four seconds of acoustic guitar that opens the album — and its twin closes it. The frame is the concept: without these two parts, the record would have no way in and no way out.
  2. 2. Dogs1:24
    Timbre: Gilmour’s guitar through the echo unit: each note spawns repeats that decay into the next phrase, so the solos sound played in a canyon. The week’s technique doing the heavy lifting — the vastness is the riff.
  3. 3. Pigs (Three Different Ones)18:29
    Rhythm: The funkiest groove on the record — bass and drums lock into a strut while Waters names names. One of the three pigs is identified outright: morality campaigner Mary Whitehouse, called out as a ‘house-proud town mouse’.
  4. 4. Sheep29:55
    Arrangement: Wright’s unaccredited electric-piano intro opens it — one of the few moments on the record that isn’t Waters’ — before the sheep rise up and the album stages its only uprising.
  5. 5. Pigs on the Wing 240:14
    Form: The same song, a different landing: part 2 is the album’s one love song, placed after forty minutes of misanthropy. The reprise is the thesis — the hope was the point all along.

ADVENTUROUS
YES 1972

Close to the Edge

Three tracks, thirty-eight minutes, zero filler — the prog album that plays like a single argument. The eighteen-minute title track is a four-movement suite with lyrics out of Hermann Hesse’s Siddhartha, assembled section by section at Advision Studios in the spring of 1972 with engineer Eddie Offord until the sessions ground down drummer Bill Bruford — he quit to join King Crimson before the album came out. ‘Siberian Khatru’ is the muscle; ‘And You and I’ is the hymn. Scores: Rolling Stone 500 #445 (2020); BestEverAlbums 85/100. The technique lives in the title track’s intro: a forty-foot tape loop of water and birdsong, two days to assemble — tape as an instrument, not an effect.

LISTEN FOR — The tape-loop intro of the title track — water, birdsong, keyboard wash on a forty-foot loop, two days to assemble — then the band crashes in on top of it. Tape as an instrument. After that, track how the four movements hand the melody off: voice, organ, guitar, voice.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Close to the Edge0:00
    Form: Four movements, 18:43 — the album’s whole argument in one track. It opens with two days of tape-loop assembly (water, birdsong, keyboard wash), then the band enters at full volume. The structure is the statement: a song can hold symphony-scale architecture.
  2. 2. And You and I18:43
    Texture: Howe’s twelve-string and Anderson’s voice carry the opening minutes alone — folk music with prog ambitions. When the full band enters, the contrast does the work: intimacy, then scale.
  3. 3. Siberian Khatru28:55
    Rhythm: The album’s one straight rocker, and even it won’t sit still — the main riff stutters in odd groupings while the drums hammer straight through. The tension between guitar and kit is the whole track.
  4. 4. Close to the Edge (2025 Steven Wilson Remix)37:53
    Space: A 2025 remix of the 1972 performance — same notes, new balance. Play the original’s organ solo, then this one: the comparison is the cue, and what the mixer foregrounds is the lesson.
  5. 5. And You and I (2025 Steven Wilson Remix)56:35
    Clarity: Same song, new mix — the vocal harmonies that blur into the original’s wash get distinct lanes here. A remix can’t change the notes; it changes what you notice first.
  6. 6. Siberian Khatru (2025 Steven Wilson Remix)66:45
    Attack: The remix treatment on the album’s hardest track — the murk lifts, the snare and bass bite. Whether it improves the song is your call; the mix is a second opinion on the same performance.
  7. 7. Close to the Edge (Instrumental)75:56
    Voicing: No Anderson — the instrumental remix hands his melodies to the organ and guitar. The suite’s architecture survives the voice’s removal; that’s how composed it is.
  8. 8. And You and I (Instrumental)94:38
    Texture: Without the vocal, the twelve-string and keyboard interplay becomes the lead voice. The song’s prettiness was never just the singing.
  9. 9. Siberian Khatru (Instrumental)104:48
    Rhythm: The riff carries the track without Anderson on top — proof the song’s engine was always the guitar-drums lock, not the melody.
  10. 10. America (Single Version)114:00
    Arrangement: A Simon & Garfunkel cover rebuilt as a prog workout — the harmonies stay, everything else gets electrified. This was their actual single; the album tracks were never going to fit on a 45.
  11. 11. Total Mass Retain (Single Version)118:10
    Form: One movement of the title track, cut for radio. It works as a standalone song — or it proves the label would try anything to get Yes on the air.
  12. 12. And You and I (Promo Radio Edit)121:28
    Density: The ten-minute hymn compressed for programmers. What survives the cut tells you what the label thought the song was: the melody, not the journey.
  13. 13. Siberia (Studio Run-Through)124:54
    Energy: The band learning ‘Siberian Khatru’ in real time — count-ins, rough edges, no varnish. The album version is a construction; this is the blueprint.
  14. 14. Close to the Edge (Rough Mix)134:13
    Space: The same performance with the polish off — instruments sit closer together, edits show. An X-ray of the finished track: you can hear the seams.
  15. 15. And You and I (Alternate Version)151:56
    Arrangement: A different pass at the same song — same notes, different decisions. Listen for what changed between this and the master; the differences are the arrangement, revealed.
  16. 16. America (Dry Mix)162:12
    Timbre: The reverb stripped off — suddenly it’s five people in a room instead of a cathedral. The room sound was doing more work than you thought.
  17. 17. Siberian Khatru (2025 Steven Wilson Edit)172:42
    Form: The full track condensed — nothing new is played, the architecture is just compressed. A different answer to what the song needs.
  18. 18. Cord of Life (Steven Wilson Edit)177:42
    Texture: The opening movement of ‘And You and I’ on its own — the folk-guitar-and-voice core, isolated. The suite’s gentlest section, unframed.
  19. 19. Total Mass Retain (Steven Wilson Edit)180:20
    Rhythm: The title track’s most driving movement, excerpted — the part where the band locks in and doesn’t let go. Momentum, bottled.
  20. 20. Siberian Khatru (Live at the Rainbow)184:34
    Tempo: Live, the tempos push — the band plays it faster and looser than the studio, holding the arrangement together in real time. No overdubs; the energy is the arrangement.
  21. 21. I’ve Seen All Good People (Live at the Rainbow)193:35
    Energy: Not a Close to the Edge track — a Fragile-era staple played the same night. The concert context: this is what Yes sounded like when the album was new.
  22. 22. Heart of the Sunrise (Live at the Rainbow)200:44
    Dynamics: Another Fragile cut, live — the quiet-loud swings hit harder on stage than on record. The band’s dynamics were a live weapon first.
  23. 23. And You and I (Live at the Rainbow)211:55
    Voicing: The hymn, live — Anderson’s voice has to carry over a real room, and the harmonies stack without studio help. The arrangement survives the translation.
  24. 24. Close to the Edge (Live at the Rainbow)221:24
    Stamina: The full eighteen minutes, live, one continuous performance — the band holding the suite’s architecture together without edits. The studio version was assembled; this one is survived.
  25. 25. Excerpts from The Six Wives of Henry VIII (Live at the Rainbow)238:43
    Timbre: Wakeman’s solo spot — keyboards alone, the band’s showpiece for its keyboardist. Prog’s cape-and-keyboards cliché, live and unapologetic.
  26. 26. Roundabout (Live at the Rainbow)245:15
    Rhythm: The hit, live — the bassline that carried Fragile, played to a room that knows every note. The crowd’s energy is part of the mix.
  27. 27. Yours is No Disgrace (Live at the Rainbow)253:24
    Energy: The Yes Album’s epic, stretched on stage — the band’s early-’70s live muscle. This is the sound the studio albums were trying to bottle.
  28. 28. Starship Trooper (Live at the Rainbow)265:00
    Form: Life Seeker into Disillusion into Würm — the three-part closer, live. The Würm section’s guitar build is the night’s payoff; Howe stretches it further than the record.

ADVENTUROUS
GENESIS 1973

Selling England by the Pound

The most English record of the week, and the Gabriel era at full power. Recorded in August 1973 at Island Studios with John Burns co-producing, it’s eight tracks alternating epics with miniatures: ‘Dancing with the Moonlit Knight’ opens on voice and twelve-string alone before the band detonates; ‘Firth of Fifth’ gives Tony Banks a minute-plus classical piano intro before Steve Hackett’s most sustained, singing guitar solo; ‘I Know What I Like’ became the band’s first UK chart single. The cover is a Betty Swanwick painting — the band had her add the lawnmower to match the song. Scores: BestEverAlbums 84/100 (1,285 votes); Rolling Stone’s #6 prog album of 2015. Where the anchor invents and Yes perfects, Genesis storytells.

LISTEN FOR — Two voices of the band in one song: on ‘Firth of Fifth’, Banks’ classical piano intro hands off to Hackett’s sustained, singing guitar lead — the composed against the improvised. The whole album alternates those two modes; this track is the clearest handoff.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Dancing with the Moonlit Knight0:00
    Dynamics: Voice and twelve-string alone for the opening minutes — then the full band detonates. The quietest-to-loudest swing on the record, and the template for everything after.
  2. 2. I Know What I Like (In Your Wardrobe)8:00
    Rhythm: The hit: a loping, almost glam-pop groove under a lawnmower-man protagonist. The chorus synth doubles the vocal melody — simplicity as a weapon on an album of complexity.
  3. 3. Firth of Fifth12:10
    Register: Banks’ piano intro runs over a minute of classical figuration before the band enters; then Hackett’s guitar sustains single notes into the stratosphere over a driving rhythm section. Composed against improvised, back to back.
  4. 4. More Fool Me21:41
    Timbre: The album’s first Collins lead vocal — a soft ballad with just acoustic guitars. The voice that would later fill stadiums is barely above a whisper here.
  5. 5. The Battle of Epping Forest24:52
    Form: Eleven minutes of character-hopping storytelling — Gabriel plays a cast of East London gangsters through musical costume changes. The most theatrical track on a theatrical album.
  6. 6. After the Ordeal36:35
    Texture: An instrumental interlude: Hackett’s classical guitar against Banks’ piano, the palate cleanser between the two epics. Four minutes of quiet the album earns.
  7. 7. The Cinema Show40:50
    Meter: The long synth-and-drums jam runs in 7/8 — count it and the groove clicks; don’t and it just feels restless. Collins’ shuffle makes the odd meter swing.
  8. 8. Aisle of Plenty51:39
    Form: Ninety seconds that reprise the opener’s melody — the bookend. The album folds back into its first song; the English theme comes full circle.

WILD CARD
VAN DER GRAAF GENERATOR 1971

Pawn Hearts

The strangest record on the syllabus, and the week’s best story. Van der Graaf Generator were a prog band built around organ, sax, and drums — no dedicated lead guitarist — plus Peter Hammill’s voice, which goes from choirboy to air-raid siren inside a single phrase. In October 1971 they cut three tracks: ‘Lemmings’, ‘Man-Erg’, and the twenty-three-minute ‘A Plague of Lighthouse Keepers’, a ten-fragment medley that plays like a drowning. Robert Fripp plays electric guitar on it — the anchor’s guitarist, moonlighting on the wildest record of the year. The album went to #1 in Italy and vanished everywhere else; the title came from saxophonist David Jackson’s slip of the tongue — ‘porn hearts’ for ‘horn parts’. Discogs 4.46/5 (2,221 collector ratings). Hammill sings like the building is on fire. Play it loud, alone, once — then decide.

LISTEN FOR — Hammill’s voice as an instrument — on ‘Lemmings’ it swings from croon to scream inside a single phrase, doing the job a lead guitar would do. Then find Fripp: his guitar enters about six minutes into ‘Man-Erg’ and about eight minutes into ‘A Plague of Lighthouse Keepers’.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Lemmings0:00
    Dynamics: Eleven minutes swinging from whisper to assault — Hammill’s voice is the lead instrument, going from croon to scream inside a single phrase. The ‘Cog’ section is the band at full pelt.
  2. 2. Man-Erg11:39
    Timbre: A piano ballad that detonates midway — drums, sax, and Fripp’s guitar entering about six minutes in. The week’s most violent shift inside one song.
  3. 3. A Plague of Lighthouse Keepers22:04
    Form: Twenty-three minutes, ten fragments — the album’s side two plays like a drowning: quiet passages, sudden storms, an ambiguous ending. Fripp’s guitar surfaces about eight minutes in.
  4. 4. Theme One45:16
    Arrangement: A George Martin BBC theme the band arranged as an instrumental — the one concise, almost-pop track in the set. The American label once slipped it onto the album without asking.
  5. 5. W (Alternate Take)48:31
    Texture: An alternate take of the ‘Theme One’ B-side — a different pass at the same material. The differences are small and telling: this is what a second attempt changes.
  6. 6. Angle of Incidents53:35
    Rhythm: One of the band’s ‘personal projects’ from the aborted double album — a free, jazzy piece. What the members wrote when nobody was watching.
  7. 7. Ponker’s Theme58:23
    Timbre: An acoustic jazz instrumental — the quietest thing in the set, and the furthest from the album’s storm. A breather that reveals the players underneath the noise.
  8. 8. Diminutions59:51
    Density: A short, dense instrumental — the band’s classical streak stated outright, voices and instruments layered into a cloud. The composed mind behind the chaos.
