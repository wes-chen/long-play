● LONG-PLAY SYLLABUS
WEEK 04 ● DIGEST

Taking the rock album apart

Concept — krautrock and motorik — 1970s West Germany’s relentless 4/4 pulse; the well OK Computer drinks from
Technique — sampling — the break, the loop, hip-hop’s founding technology
ANCHOR
RADIOHEAD 2000

Kid A

The biggest band in the world finished a guitar masterpiece, looked at their instruments, and put them down. This is five men deciding the old tools couldn’t say what they needed.

Verdict: the anchor — the 2000s’ most influential album, and proof a rock band could survive by becoming an electronic one.

Scores: Pitchfork 10/10 — the rare perfect score, from Brent DiCrescenzo; Rolling Stone, Pitchfork, and The Times all named it the #1 album of the 2000s; Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums placed it #20 in 2020; Grammys: Best Alternative Album win, Album of the Year nomination. CULT evidence pending — researched live.

The moment: after OK Computer, Yorke burned out — “a complete fucking mess … completely unhinged” — and froze in front of every instrument. He started listening almost exclusively to Warp’s electronic artists, Aphex Twin and Autechre: voiceless structures that moved him as much as guitar music ever had.

Lineage: the sources are worn openly — Warp IDM, 1970s krautrock (Can), the jazz of Mingus, Coltrane, and Davis; Yorke called Talking Heads’ Remain in Light a “massive reference point,” and Björk’s Homogenic is named as a direct influence — it sits in this week’s syllabus too.

Then vs now: on release the new sound divided listeners — some dismissed it as pretentious and wilfully obscure. By decade’s end the argument was over: #1 of the 2000s everywhere that counted.

Why you: your two island poles — Wish You Were Here and OK Computer — both point here. It’s the rock album taken apart and rebuilt by a sampler. If you press play on one record this week, this is the one.

LISTEN FOR — The sample. On ‘Idioteque,’ the drums and bass loop a 1973 computer-music recording — a drum kit nobody played. Track how often this week’s records build grooves out of lifted sound instead of played instruments.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Everything In Its Right Place0:00
    Texture: Yorke’s voice is looped and re-pitched into a choir that never fully resolves. The first sound on the album is a singer turned into a sample — the week’s technique, stated in the opening minute.
  2. 2. Kid A4:11
    Timbre: a music-box synth and a stuttering drum loop carry a voice pushed so far down it reads as text, not song. The drums sound chopped; the melody sounds remembered, not performed.
  3. 3. The National Anthem8:55
    Rhythm: a two-note bassline that refuses to change — motorik’s whole philosophy in four strings — while a horn section storms on top. The week’s concept, wearing a jazz band’s clothes.
  4. 4. How to Disappear Completely14:47
    Dynamics: strings swell and vanish without ever resolving while the voice stays pinned to center. The orchestra does the shouting; the singer doesn’t.
  5. 5. Treefingers20:43
    Timbre: guitars fed through processing until they lose all attack — a guitar that refuses to sound like a guitar. Three and a half minutes of an instrument forgetting what it is.
  6. 6. Optimistic24:26
    Rhythm: the closest thing to a rock song here, and even its drums keep breaking into chopped fills. The groove keeps threatening to become normal and never does.
  7. 7. In Limbo29:42
    Texture: guitars phase and overlap, melodies doubling at strange intervals, while the rhythm section holds dead center. The stereo field pulls apart and the song happens in the gap.
  8. 8. Idioteque33:13
    Sampling: the drums and bass are lifted from a 1973 computer-music composition by Paul Lansky — a drum kit nobody played. Two musicians, one sample, the week’s technique at full power.
  9. 9. Morning Bell38:22
    Form: the song loops a single progression and changes everything around it — each pass adds a layer, subtracts a voice, and the ending arrives without a chorus ever happening.
  10. 10. Motion Picture Soundtrack42:58
    Timbre: a pedal organ and harp samples aiming at 1950s Disney — the most artificial sound on the record, placed at the end like a lullaby from a machine.
  11. 11. Untitled46:18
    Dynamics: fifty-two seconds of synth wash after the album’s end. Not a song — a room emptying out.

ADVENTUROUS
TALK TALK 1991

Laughing Stock

A band famous for synth-pop hits walked away from its label, locked itself in a darkened studio for a year with fifty musicians, and erased 80% of what they played. What survived became the blueprint for post-rock.

Verdict: the adventurous one — the quietest, most disciplined record this week, and the one everything else here descends from.

Scores: Pitchfork’s 2011 retrospective gave it 10/10 and named it the #11 album of the 1990s; Stylus called it the greatest post-rock album in 2007. CULT evidence pending — researched live.

The moment: after an acrimonious split from EMI, Talk Talk signed to Polydor’s Verve and recorded from September 1990 to April 1991 at Wessex with producer Tim Friese-Greene and engineer Phill Brown — clocks removed, windows blacked out, oil projectors on the walls. Around fifty musicians played; only eighteen made the final cut. Brown’s rule: it takes “strong discipline to erase 80% of the music you record.”

Lineage: Hollis named his honesty standards — Can’s Tago Mago, Coltrane’s “In a Sentimental Mood,” Dylan’s New Morning — and his silence doctrine: “I would rather hear one note than I would two, and I would rather hear silence than I would one note.”

Then vs now: it sank on release in September 1991; now it’s the genre’s founding document. The connection runs straight into this week’s syllabus — FACT later called Field of Reeds “a successor of Talk Talk’s majestic strand of post-rock.”

Why you: the week’s patience test. And it repays twice — Field of Reeds is in direct conversation with it, so the two albums read each other.

LISTEN FOR — The silence between sounds. Laughing Stock is built from hours of improvisation erased down to single gestures — track how long the band lets a note decay before anything else happens.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. The Rainbow0:00
    Dynamics: the album starts in near-silence — mumbled guitar figures, brushes — and lets the noise floor into the arrangement. When the full band finally enters, the quiet has taught you how to listen.
  2. 2. Eden9:09
    Texture: organ and horns rise like weather; instruments enter one at a time and get subtracted just as deliberately. The erasure method, working in real time.
  3. 3. Desire15:43
    Rhythm: drums, bass, and guitar each seem to follow a different pulse, then lock for a few bars and let go. A groove that keeps slipping out of its own hands.
  4. 4. Inheritance22:40
    Timbre: Hollis’s voice at its most exposed — cracked, close-miked, barely above a whisper — against chords that decay for seconds. Vulnerability as an arrangement choice.
  5. 5. I Believe in You28:04
    Form: a slow build that refuses every chorus-shaped resolution. Six minutes of approach with no arrival; the harmonica drone at the end just stops.
  6. 6. Wealth34:14
    Dynamics: guitar harmonics and almost nothing else. The record’s final statement is restraint — the quietest album ever made that goes this far out.

ADVENTUROUS
BJÖRK 1997

Homogenic

She left a tabloid storm in London, ended the most successful production partnership of her career, and made an album out of two ingredients: volcanic beats and an Icelandic string octet.

Verdict: the adventurous one — the record that proves an electronic album can hit like a symphony and a symphony can move like a club track.

Scores: Pitchfork gave it 9.9/10 in 1997, then a perfect 10 on the 2017 re-review; Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums placed it #202 in 2020. CULT evidence pending — researched live.

The moment: London sessions were halted after the suicide of her stalker turned her home into a media siege; she relocated to El Cortijo in Málaga, Spain, and recorded the album there. She cut ties with producer Nellee Hooper — they’d “stopped surprising each other” — and built the record around Mark Bell’s beats and the Icelandic String Octet.

Lineage: Björk wanted it to sound like “rough volcanoes with soft moss growing all over it.” Her own formula: “The electronic beats are the rhythm, the heartbeat. The violins create the old-fashioned atmosphere, the colouring.” Iceland’s contradiction — volcanic earth, hi-tech culture — is the album’s thesis.

Then vs now: acclaimed on release, and still climbing — Rolling Stone’s 500 list didn’t exist for the 1997 press; by 2020 the album sat at #202, and Pitchfork upgraded its score twenty years later.

Why you: Radiohead named Homogenic as an influence on Kid A, so the anchor has a parent in the room. It’s also the most direct bridge here between the electronic and the orchestral — everything Field of Reeds attempts sixteen years later starts with this collision.

LISTEN FOR — The standoff. On ‘Jóga,’ Mark Bell’s hard programmed beats collide with string swells that refuse to soften — two kinds of power, no compromise.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Hunter0:00
    Rhythm: hard electronic beats under a vocal line that surges like a search. The strings enter later as counterweight — the whole song is a standoff between machine pulse and orchestra.
  2. 2. Jóga4:13
    Texture: the album’s thesis in one track — volcanic string swells colliding with programmed beats. The octet and the electronics share the space without blending.
  3. 3. Unravel9:18
    Timbre: a music-box melody over a deep, heavy low end. The smallest sound on the record carries the most weight.
  4. 4. Bachelorette12:36
    Form: a three-act crescendo — whispered opening, string-laden middle, full orchestral climax. The album’s biggest structure, built from the simplest materials.
  5. 5. All Neon Like17:53
    Timbre: harp-like plucks and glassy percussion around a slow, heavy groove. The decorations outrun the rhythm — everything sparkles except the beat.
  6. 6. 5 Years23:46
    Rhythm: the beats turn aggressive — a breakbeat stomp under one of the harshest vocals on the record. The album’s anger, stated plainly.
  7. 7. Immature28:15
    Dynamics: a slow-burn string arrangement that never releases. The tension is the point; there is no payoff and none is promised.
  8. 8. Alarm Call31:21
    Rhythm: the album’s one dancefloor moment — a skipping beat that actually swings. After seven tracks of gravity, relief.
  9. 9. Pluto35:41
    Timbre: everything pushed into the red — distorted, overloaded beats for two minutes straight. The loudest the album gets, and it sounds like it hurts.
  10. 10. All Is Full of Love39:00
    Texture: the beats dissolve into a wash of synths. After nine tracks of collision, the album closes on fusion — the standoff ends in synthesis.

ADVENTUROUS
THESE NEW PURITANS 2013

Field of Reeds

A band that made its name with dancehall and Taiko drums fired its keyboardist, hired an orchestra, and made an album where the rock band is a guest in its own songs.

Verdict: the adventurous one — Laughing Stock’s erasure method applied to classical forces, and the gentlest entry point in a loud week.

Scores: Pitchfork 8.4/10 (Laura Snapes); Metacritic 82/100, “universal acclaim” from 26 critics; FACT’s 2016 best-post-rock list: #26, calling it “one of only a few records that could claim to be a successor of Talk Talk’s majestic strand of post-rock.” CULT evidence pending — researched live.

The moment: after Hidden’s electronic dancehall, Jack Barnett rebuilt the band around classical forces — recording in Berlin, London, and Tetbury with over thirty-eight session musicians across two ensembles: the Stargaze Ensemble and the Synergy Vocal Ensemble. Produced by Barnett and Graham Sutton; released June 2013 on Infectious.

Lineage: it opens with someone else’s song — Bacharach and David’s “This Guy’s in Love with You” — sung straight, as if to say the orchestra is the band now. The Laughing Stock comparison is the critical consensus.

Then vs now: acclaimed on release but a commercial non-event (#90 UK). Three years later FACT put it in the post-rock canon proper — the slow-burn reassessment arc.

Why you: this is the week’s proof of method. Laughing Stock showed how erasure could build an album; Field of Reeds shows the same instincts rebuilt for an orchestra. And it’s the record you’ll reach for first — nothing here is trying to hurt you.

LISTEN FOR — The missing guitar. On ‘Fragment Two,’ piano, voice, and ensemble swells build the pressure a riff used to build — track what the orchestra does that distortion used to do.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. This Guy’s In Love with You0:00
    Form: a Bacharach standard sung straight, orchestral sweetness and all. The album opens with someone else’s song — the sincerity disarms you before the strangeness starts.
  2. 2. Fragment Two3:02
    Texture: piano and voice against a string ensemble swelling in irregular waves. The rock band is gone; chamber forces do the heavy lifting.
  3. 3. The Light in Your Name7:33
    Dynamics: the record’s loudest eruption — brass and strings building to a controlled detonation, then silence. Six minutes, one explosion.
  4. 4. V (Island Song)13:36
    Form: nine minutes that cycle through movements without repeating a section — the rock album’s verse-chorus machinery taken apart and left in pieces.
  5. 5. Spiral22:53
    Rhythm: a repeating piano figure spinning in place while the orchestra orbits. Motorik by way of minimalism — the week’s concept, taught by a piano loop.
  6. 6. Organ Eternal28:56
    Timbre: Rhodes and organ tones layered into a drone that barely moves. The title is the instruction manual.
  7. 7. Nothing Else34:28
    Texture: nearly eight minutes of near-stillness — voice, strings, space. The album’s quiet core; everything the record knows about restraint in one track.
  8. 8. Dream42:17
    Dynamics: a gentle swell that never breaks. The closest the record comes to a lullaby — and the only track that lets you relax.
  9. 9. Field of Reeds46:32
    Form: the title track stacks everything the album taught — voice, strings, brass, piano — into one final assembly. The reassembly, after the disassembly.

WILD CARD
BLACK MIDI 2022

Hellfire

Three albums in three years, then the band quit at the peak. This is their finale — 39 minutes of first-person villains, boxing matches, and horse races, played by people who treat time signatures as suggestions.

Verdict: the wild card — pure velocity and story. Where the rest of the week disassembles the rock album with patience, Hellfire does it with a sledgehammer.

Scores: Pitchfork 7.8/10; Metacritic 79/100 (“generally favorable”); Exclaim! and The Line of Best Fit went 9/10, Paste 8.6/10. CULT evidence pending — researched live.

The moment: their third album in three years, recorded mostly in thirteen days with producer Marta Salogni. Greep: “If Cavalcade was a drama, Hellfire is like an epic action film” — each song a first-person tale from one of his “scumbags.” It entered the UK chart at #22, their highest placing; two years later the band announced an indefinite hiatus.

Lineage: the collisions are the sound — math-rock, show tunes, flamenco, country, and cabaret inside single songs; AllMusic’s Paul Simpson heard Mr. Bungle and Fred Frith more than the King Crimson-isms of their past.

Then vs now: no slow reassessment arc — it’s the final studio album, and the 2024 hiatus froze it as the last word. A band that burned through its whole discography in four years and walked away.

Why you: the wild card converts on story, not specs — and the story is a band playing like the building’s on fire. If the week’s quieter records don’t land, this one reminds you what velocity feels like. Wild-card reactions count double.

LISTEN FOR — The tempo changes. On ‘Sugar/Tzu’ the band shifts gear mid-phrase while the vocal narrates a boxing match — count how many speeds one song survives.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Hellfire0:00
    Timbre: a string quartet playing something uneasy for eighty-four seconds. Classical instruments set the tone; the band hasn’t even arrived yet.
  2. 2. Sugar/Tzu1:24
    Rhythm: tempo changes stacked on tempo changes — the band shifts gear mid-phrase while the vocal narrates a boxing match. The drums chase a voice that won’t sit still.
  3. 3. Eat Men Eat5:14
    Timbre: a queasy synth line and a bass groove that lurches. The flamenco-inflected prog turns sour on purpose — everything here is slightly wrong, deliberately.
  4. 4. Welcome to Hell8:23
    Rhythm: the one steady groove on the album — almost funky — under a soldier’s desertion story. The catchiest thing here is also the most sinister.
  5. 5. Still12:32
    Dynamics: nearly six minutes of slow burn from the loudest band in the room. The tension never pays off, and that’s the point.
  6. 6. Half Time18:19
    Form: twenty-six seconds of horn fragment. An intermission that divides the record in two.
  7. 7. The Race Is About To Begin18:45
    Rhythm: seven minutes of stop-start math-rock — the band plays horse-racing commentary as percussion. The album’s centerpiece, and its longest sprint.
  8. 8. Dangerous Liaisons26:00
    Timbre: the guitar work turns glassy and precise. The most technically dazzling track, and the coldest.
  9. 9. The Defence30:15
    Dynamics: three minutes at full sprint — no build, no release, just velocity.
  10. 10. 27 Questions33:14
    Form: the finale assembles the album’s tricks — spoken-word narration, tempo shifts — and lets the chaos resolve into something like triumph.
