WEEK 02 ● DIGEST

The album as a designed object

Concept — groove and the pocket — rhythm is the pattern on paper; groove is humans agreeing how far behind the beat to sit
Technique — panning/stereo image — where each sound sits left-to-right; headphones required.
ANCHOR
RADIOHEAD 1997

OK Computer

You know this record — now hear it as a construction site. Chopped drums, stone-staircase reverb, and a pocket the band built in the edit instead of playing.

LISTEN FOR — The pocket is built, not found — on ‘Airbag’ the drums are chopped and reassembled to land just behind where your body expects; compare that designed lateness to the live-band push of ‘Electioneering.’
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Airbag0:00
    Texture: the drums sound sliced — hits start and stop too cleanly, like tape cut with a razor. The bass lumbers a half-step behind them. Nobody is "in the pocket"; the pocket was assembled, and the unease is the point.
  2. 2. Paranoid Android4:47
    Form: four songs stitched end to end — acoustic lament, sludge-metal detour, choral lullaby. The groove never carries across the seams; each section resets the pulse, so the whiplash is the structure.
  3. 3. Subterranean Homesick Alien11:15
    Panning: guitars hover at the far edges, left and right, while the voice sits dead center and dry. The stereo image is a room you float inside — wide at the walls, empty in the middle.
  4. 4. Exit Music (For A Film)15:42
    Dynamics: a minute of near-silence — voice and a few guitar notes — then bass and drums detonate at once. The band refuses to rush the build; they sit behind the pulse until the song shoves them forward.
  5. 5. Let Down20:09
    Texture: two guitars arpeggiate the same figure, panned hard left and right, slightly out of sync with each other. The drums underneath are deliberately plain — all the motion is in the overlap at the edges.
  6. 6. Karma Police25:09
    Meter: straight 4/4, piano chords on the beat, the most conventional groove on the record. After five tracks of unease it feels almost suspiciously normal — the simplicity is doing the emotional work.
  7. 7. Fitter Happier29:33
    Timbre: a flat synthetic voice reads a checklist over a twitching loop. Nothing breathes; the "groove" is a machine grid, perfectly on time, and the rigidity is both the joke and the threat.
  8. 8. Electioneering31:30
    Rhythm: the one track that plays it fast and straight — guitars up front, drums driving instead of floating. The pocket leans forward here; everything else on the record leans back.
  9. 9. Climbing Up the Walls35:21
    Dynamics: the beat barely moves for four minutes while strings, noise, and drums pile on under a whisper. The groove holds its breath — the pressure comes from everything stacking, not from speeding up.
  10. 10. No Surprises40:06
    Timbre: glockenspiel and clean guitar, close and narrow in the stereo image — everything huddles near the center. The prettiest, smallest sound on the record carrying the bleakest lyric.
  11. 11. Lucky43:55
    Form: one long crescendo — verse, lift, fall back, lift higher. The rhythm section keeps agreeing to push a little harder without ever breaking tempo; the wave shape is the whole song.
  12. 12. The Tourist48:14
    Dynamics: the drums finally shuffle instead of drive. After eleven tracks of tension the beat goes slack on purpose — slower, looser, the record letting go.

ADVENTUROUS
THE BEATLES 1969

Abbey Road

The last thing four people recorded together, sequenced like a goodbye. Ringo’s pocket is the week’s concept in miniature: drums so far behind the beat they feel lazy, but never drag.

LISTEN FOR — Ringo’s pocket: on ‘Come Together’ the drums sit so far behind the beat they feel lazy but never drag — that agreed lateness is the week’s concept, and this is the first Beatles album never issued in mono, so the stereo field is the only field.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Come Together0:00
    Rhythm: Ringo's drums land a fraction behind every beat — lazy, but never late enough to drag. That gap between the pulse and the hit is the week's concept in one bar: groove as agreed lateness.
  2. 2. Something4:19
    Panning: headphones on — drums hard to one side, bass to the other, the 1969 stereo mix. The wide split lets you hear each player's lateness separately; the groove is unhurried and you can audit it per ear.
  3. 3. Maxwell's Silver Hammer7:22
    Rhythm: a bouncy, square music-hall pocket that refuses to swing. The joke works because the band plays it dead straight — the groove is the straight man and the lyric is the comic.
  4. 4. Oh! Darling10:50
    Timbre: McCartney's vocal is shredded at the top of its range, straining against a backbeat that won't move. The pocket stays put; the voice fights it, and the friction is the song.
  5. 5. Octopus's Garden14:17
    Texture: bubbling guitar effects drift across the stereo field, everything soft-edged and wet. The groove paddles instead of driving — Ringo's song gets Ringo's pocket: genial, behind the beat, unbothered.
  6. 6. I Want You (She's So Heavy)17:08
    Dynamics: one riff repeated until it turns physical, then organ and white noise bury it. Everyone leans forward together — and the track doesn't end, it gets switched off mid-grind, which is a different kind of finality.
  7. 7. Here Comes the Sun24:55
    Meter: the verses slip between time signatures without telling you. Don't count it — feel how the groove keeps refreshing itself instead of settling; the brightness is the pattern refusing to sit still.
  8. 8. Because28:01
    Panning: nine voices, triple-tracked, spread left to right like furniture. No drums, no groove at all — proof the stereo image alone can carry a track when the placement is this deliberate.
  9. 9. You Never Give Me Your Money30:46
    Form: three grooves in one song — piano ballad, honky-tonk lope, guitar rocker. A miniature of the whole side-two method: change the pocket and the same song becomes a different room.
  10. 10. Sun King34:49
    Texture: guitars and voices soaked in reverb, wide and soft. The beat barely exists — the track floats, and the stereo wash does the work the rhythm section isn't.
  11. 11. Mean Mr Mustard37:16
    Rhythm: short, fast, slightly rushed — the pocket hurries. At barely a minute it's a corridor, not a room: the groove changes just to keep the medley walking.
  12. 12. Polythene Pam38:22
    Dynamics: loud, fast, over in a flash. The band attacks it like a live take — forward, aggressive, a sprint wedged between two strolls.
  13. 13. She Came in Through the Bathroom Window39:35
    Rhythm: the tempo settles and the groove loosens after two sprints. Same band, same session — but sitting back instead of pushing makes them sound like a different group.
  14. 14. Golden Slumbers41:33
    Dynamics: piano and voice alone, then the full band lifts it. The drums don't enter until late — and their entrance is the emotional event of the track, not the lyric.
  15. 15. Carry That Weight43:05
    Texture: everyone singing at once, brass filling the stereo image wall to wall. The groove turns into a march — steady, communal, the pocket as shared labor.
  16. 16. The End44:42
    Panning: the drum solo, then three guitar solos trading lines left, right, center. The week's technique made literal — stereo placement as conversation between players.
  17. 17. Her Majesty47:03
    Form: one guitar, one voice, dead center. After the wall of sound the stereo image collapses to a single point — the designed object ends with a doodle.

KENDRICK LAMAR 2015

To Pimp a Butterfly

The decade’s biggest rap album, played by a live jazz band with no grid. The pocket here is human — push, pull, and breath — and that’s the whole lesson.

LISTEN FOR — Live, not programmed: ‘The Blacker the Berry’ runs on real drums — hear the push-and-pull against the bass that no grid would allow, then stack it against the machine-locked strut of ‘King Kunta.’
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Wesley's Theory0:00
    Texture: a live funk band and a rap vocal in the same room, bleeding into each other's mics. The drums push and pull against the bass instead of locking to a grid — the raggedness is the humanity the album is selling.
  2. 2. For Free? - Interlude4:47
    Rhythm: Kendrick raps over free-jazz piano with no fixed pulse. The "groove" is speech rhythm — the pocket lands wherever his breath lands, and the band chases him.
  3. 3. King Kunta6:58
    Rhythm: the bassline sits so far back it feels like it's dragging the song behind it. Pure Parliament lean — everyone agrees to arrive late, and the lateness is the funk.
  4. 4. Institutionalized10:52
    Dynamics: the beat stays cool and mid-tempo while the vocal gets more frantic. The pocket refuses to match the panic — the groove holds steady so the words sound more desperate by contrast.
  5. 5. These Walls15:24
    Panning: backing vocals stacked wide while Kendrick stays close and centered. The stereo image is a room full of voices around one confession — the placement is doing narrative work.
  6. 6. u20:25
    Timbre: the voice cracks, doubles, slurs — recorded to sound like it's coming apart in real time. The pocket stumbles with it; when the narrator breaks down, the groove is allowed to break too.
  7. 7. Alright24:54
    Rhythm: the hook marches, the verses float. The pocket does two jobs — anthem when the crowd needs one, loose talk when Kendrick needs to think out loud.
  8. 8. For Sale? - Interlude28:33
    Texture: glassy synths, the vocal multi-tracked and slippery. The groove is deliberately easy to fall into — the pocket is the temptation, which is the point of the scene.
  9. 9. Momma33:25
    Meter: live drums with room sound, swinging warm and unhurried. After the middle stretch's tension the pocket exhales — the groove relaxes like shoulders dropping.
  10. 10. Hood Politics38:08
    Rhythm: harder drums, faster tongue — the pocket tightens and leans forward. The band plays it like they have somewhere to be; the urgency is in the tempo, not the volume.
  11. 11. How Much A Dollar Cost43:00
    Dynamics: the beat stays gentle and round while the story gets heavier. The pocket is soft on purpose — the groove cradles a parable instead of hammering it.
  12. 12. Complexion (A Zulu Love)47:22
    Texture: Rhodes, live bass, harmonies spread across the field. The most relaxed pocket on the record — nobody hurries, and the glow comes from the unhurriedness.
  13. 13. The Blacker The Berry51:45
    Dynamics: boom-bap drums detonate after the softness before them. The pocket goes rigid and militant — the groove stops swaying and starts marching.
  14. 14. You Ain't Gotta Lie (Momma Said)57:14
    Rhythm: a loping, confident pocket right after the fury. The groove struts — and the sequencing is the statement: anger, then swagger, in that order.
  15. 15. i61:16
    Form: the crowd enters the track — call and response, hands in the air. The pocket stops belonging to the studio and belongs to the room.
  16. 16. Mortal Man66:52
    Form: the beat fades and a staged interview with Tupac takes over. The pocket dissolves into speech — the designed object ends by breaking its own form.

ADVENTUROUS
SZA 2022

SOS

A hundred songs written, twenty-three kept, and the sequencing holds the sprawl together. Vocal stacks as architecture: map where each voice lives left-to-right.

LISTEN FOR — Vocal stacks as architecture — the backing voices on ‘Snooze’ are tracked wide and mixed into a carved pocket; map where each voice lives left-to-right, then hunt the buried Lizzo harmony hidden in ‘F2F.’
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. SOS0:00
    Timbre: a siren-like vocal over a dark, sparse beat. The pocket is coiled — the groove holds back, promising a bigger album than any one song.
  2. 2. Kill Bill1:57
    Rhythm: mid-tempo, uncluttered, almost plain — the pocket deliberately stays out of the melody's way. That's why the hook lives in your head for days: nothing competes with it.
  3. 3. Seek & Destroy4:31
    Dynamics: the beat simmers while the vocal stacks pile up. The pocket withholds its release — the groove waits until the harmonies force its hand.
  4. 4. Low7:55
    Rhythm: trap drums sitting low in the mix, loose and unhurried. The beat leans back so the vocal can lean forward — the pocket makes room instead of taking it.
  5. 5. Love Language10:56
    Texture: soft synths, rounded edges, vocals layered like cloth. The pocket cushions rather than drives — the groove is upholstery.
  6. 6. Blind14:00
    Panning: the vocal doubled and spread wide until the stereo image blurs. The pocket drifts slightly out of focus — the groove sounds uncertain on purpose, matching the lyric.
  7. 7. Used (feat. Don Toliver)16:31
    Timbre: two voices trading verses over a muted beat — SZA's warm stack against Don Toliver's thinner tone. The pocket stays small so the contrast between the voices fills the space.
  8. 8. Snooze18:58
    Rhythm: the most effortless pocket on the record — the beat barely seems to try. The groove is so natural it disappears, which is why the song feels inevitable instead of constructed.
  9. 9. Notice Me22:19
    Rhythm: the vocal rushes and tugs against a beat that won't budge. The pocket holds firm while the voice strains at it — that tension is the song's actual subject.
  10. 10. Gone Girl25:00
    Dynamics: the beat thins as the song goes, instruments dropping out one by one. The groove lets go gradually — the arrangement performs the leaving.
  11. 11. Smoking on my Ex Pack29:04
    Form: ninety seconds, almost a skit. The pocket appears just long enough to make its point and vanishes — the groove as a shrug.
  12. 12. Ghost in the Machine (feat. Phoebe Bridgers)30:28
    Timbre: two vocal textures in one stereo space — SZA's warm stack, Phoebe Bridgers' cool whisper. The pocket is shared carefully; neither voice crowds the other.
  13. 13. F2F34:07
    Timbre: pop-punk guitars crash into an R&B record. The pocket goes rigid and driving — the groove changes genre mid-album, which is the sequencing making its argument out loud.
  14. 14. Nobody Gets Me37:12
    Dynamics: guitar and voice, the beat nearly gone. After F2F's noise the groove exhales — the quiet does the emotional work the drums were doing.
  15. 15. Conceited40:13
    Rhythm: the beat bounces, unhurried and sure of itself. The pocket struts — the groove walks the way the title talks.
  16. 16. Special42:44
    Timbre: close-miked vocal, every breath audible, the beat soft underneath. The pocket handles the song gently — the groove treats it like something that bruises.
  17. 17. Too Late45:23
    Panning: backing vocals pushed to the edges, the lead small and centered. The stereo image maps the lyric — closeness in the middle, everything else far away.
  18. 18. Far48:07
    Rhythm: a slow, heavy pocket — the beat drags its feet. The lateness the week celebrates curdles here into the sound of giving up.
  19. 19. Shirt51:08
    Texture: hazy synths, the vocal half-buried in the mix. The pocket obscures as much as it reveals — the murk is the appeal, not an accident.
  20. 20. Open Arms (feat. Travis Scott)54:10
    Dynamics: the arrangement blooms in the chorus, voices multiplying. The pocket widens — the groove makes room for two people instead of one.
  21. 21. I Hate U58:09
    Timbre: the vocal sounds like a phone recording — small, compressed, close. The pocket is minimal; the groove gets out of the way of a message that was never meant to be a song.
  22. 22. Good Days61:03
    Form: a guitar loop circling without resolving. The pocket is patient to the point of stillness — the groove meditates instead of arriving.
  23. 23. Forgiveless (feat. Ol' Dirty Bastard)65:43
    Form: Ol' Dirty Bastard crashes the finale. The pocket fractures — the groove breaks its own rules at the finish line.

WILD CARD
SWANS 2014

To Be Kind

Two hours of one band playing the same riffs until the room surrenders. Repetition as groove — the pocket deepens instead of developing, until it becomes ritual.

LISTEN FOR — Repetition as groove: ‘Screen Shot’ hammers one idea until the pocket deepens instead of developing — the repetition is the content, and the groove becomes ritual.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Screen Shot0:00
    Rhythm: one chord hammered for eight minutes over a motorik pulse. The pocket doesn't develop — it deepens, and the repetition becomes the entire content.
  2. 2. Just A Little Boy (For Chester Burnett)8:04
    Dynamics: a slow swell — quiet, loud, quiet, louder. The band moves as one mass; nobody solos, everybody surges, and the pocket is collective or it's nothing.
  3. 3. A Little God In My Hands20:44
    Texture: handclaps, shouts, and a lurching riff piled into a wall. The groove stumbles on purpose — the pocket trips and the trip is the dance.
  4. 4. Bring The Sun/Toussaint L'Ouverture27:52
    Form: two halves, one argument — the first builds a single riff into a roar, the second releases it. The pocket wins by refusing to stop.
  5. 5. Some Things We Do61:57
    Dynamics: after an hour of volume, near-silence — hushed voices, no drums. The pocket vanishes; the groove becomes breath, and the contrast re-tunes your ears for what's next.
  6. 6. She Loves Us67:06
    Rhythm: the riff returns slower and heavier. The pocket drags — the week's celebrated lateness curdles into menace.
  7. 7. Kirsten Supine84:07
    Timbre: wordless vocals layered over a grinding pulse. The groove repeats until meaning drains out and only physical feeling is left — ritual, not song.
  8. 8. Oxygen94:40
    Dynamics: the album's loudest stretch — drums and guitars at full force, every player hitting the same point as hard as possible. Violent agreement.
  9. 9. Nathalie Neal102:39
    Texture: a single idea stretched until it shimmers. The groove barely moves — the pocket is a held breath, and the tension is the content.
  10. 10. To Be Kind112:54
    Form: a slow, hymn-like resolution. After two hours the pocket finally relaxes — the groove forgives, and the title lands as an instruction.
