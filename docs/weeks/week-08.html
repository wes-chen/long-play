WEEK 08 ● DIGEST

Kraftwerk week

Concept — context as appreciation — 1977 Düsseldorf inventing the future with machines; the Kraftwerk law in action
Technique — sequencers and synthesizers — pop built note-by-note from circuits
ANCHOR
KRAFTWERK 1977

Trans-Europe Express

Four men in suits stood on a railway bridge, listened to a real train, and decided it wasn’t danceable enough. So they built a better one out of circuits.

Verdict: the anchor — the record this whole week is built around: pop music assembled note by note from machines, and the blueprint for everything after it.

Scores: AllMusic 5/5 (the reference guide’s full marks). Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums, 2020 edition: No. 238. Pitchfork’s No. 6 album of the 1970s. Slant’s No. 1 electronic album of the 20th century.

The moment: recorded in 1976 at Kling Klang Studio in Düsseldorf and released in March 1977. The band’s new instrument was the Synthanorma, a custom 32-step sequencer that let them build lines no hand could hold steady. They recorded two complete versions, one in English and one in German. They went to railway bridges to hear real trains and found the sound not danceable, so they changed it slightly. The train was the point: a band that grew up in postwar Germany, with no pop culture of its own, chose the Trans Europ Express as a symbol of a new European identity.

Lineage: the clean, melodic machine-pop that every synth record since is measured against. Slant called its influence “unprecedented,” reaching from Radiohead’s Kid A to Afrika Bambaataa’s “Planet Rock” — the title track’s melody became the basis of hip-hop’s founding electro record.

Then vs now: it barely charted in America and finished No. 30 in the Village Voice’s 1977 Pazz & Jop poll. Then it grew: maximum ratings from AllMusic, Mojo, and Rolling Stone, and in 2014 the Los Angeles Times called it “the most important pop album of the last 40 years.”

Why you: the week’s concept in one record — context as appreciation. If the machines sound cold at first, the train story is the door in.

LISTEN FOR — Follow the sequencer. The title track’s pulse never wavers while the melody rides on top of it — listen for the moment the machine starts sounding like a train, and the train starts sounding like a machine.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Europe Endless0:00
    Rhythm: a steady sequenced pulse carries a simple synth melody that repeats with small changes. The pattern never rushes, and that steadiness is what makes it feel like a train.
  2. 2. The Hall of Mirrors9:41
    Timbre: a cold, glassy synth line against a deadpan vocal. The melody sounds like it is being read off a mirror.
  3. 3. Showroom Dummies17:37
    Melody: the catchiest tune on the record, bouncy where the rest is stately. This is the pop song hiding inside the concept album.
  4. 4. Trans-Europe Express23:52
    Texture: the chant of the title phrase layers over the chugging rhythm one voice at a time. The track builds by adding, never by speeding up.
  5. 5. Metal on Metal30:30
    Rhythm: metallic clangs land on the beat like a hammer on rails. The shortest track on the record, and the hardest sound on it.
  6. 6. Abzug32:41
    Dynamics: the suite exhales. The pulse thins out and the melody drifts, like a train pulling away from the station.
  7. 7. Franz Schubert37:35
    Melody: a slow, wistful synth line that sounds like a hymn played by a machine. The sequencer’s most elaborate pattern on the record.
  8. 8. Endless Endless42:01
    Form: fifty-five seconds of the opening theme returning as a coda. The record ends by looping back to its start.

ADVENTUROUS
NEU! 1973

Neu! 2

The first side is eleven minutes of one drumbeat that never changes. The second side is the same two songs played at the wrong speed. Both halves are the point.

Verdict: the adventurous one — the rawest version of the week’s idea: two men, one beat, and a budget that ran out halfway through.

Scores: Pitchfork 7.5/10 (the indie review publication; the 2001 reissue review). AllMusic 4/5 (the reference guide). Paul Morley put it on his 100 Greatest Albums of All Time list.

The moment: recorded in January 1973 in Hamburg with producer Conny Plank. Michael Rother and Klaus Dinger had both come out of Kraftwerk and left to make something faster and looser. The money ran out after side one, so side two is their single “Neuschnee/Super” manipulated on a record player and a cassette recorder — sped up to 78 rpm, slowed to 16. Critics at the time dismissed it as a cheap gimmick. Dinger later called it a prototype of the multiple remixes that come with pop singles now.

Lineage: the motorik beat — one unchanging drum pattern, driving forward — that runs through post-punk, indie, and half of modern rock. “Super 16” turned up in Kill Bill. The New York Times called the three Neu! records “landmarks of German experimental rock.”

Then vs now: dismissed as a trick in 1973; now the trick reads as the idea. The record that couldn’t afford a second side invented the remix album by accident.

Why you: the week’s technique at its most stripped down. If the sequencer is the machine version, motorik is the human version — a drummer playing like a machine, and a band that ran out of money and made the shortage the record.

LISTEN FOR — Track the motorik beat. One unchanging drum pattern drives “Für Immer” for eleven minutes while the guitars layer on top — then notice side two is the same two songs sped up and slowed down.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Fur immer0:00
    Rhythm: one drum pattern, unchanged for eleven minutes, while guitars stack in layers. The beat never varies, and that is the whole idea.
  2. 2. Spitzenqualitat11:17
    Timbre: a short burst of fuzzed guitar over the same driving beat. The riff sounds like it is being played through a broken speaker.
  3. 3. Gedenkminute (fuer A+K)14:52
    Dynamics: two minutes of near-silence after the rush. A minute of remembrance, literally — the record stops to breathe.
  4. 4. Lila Engel (Lilac Angel)16:58
    Voice: Dinger’s voice enters, half-spoken, half-sung, over a beat that keeps walking. The machines sound human for four minutes.
  5. 5. Neuschnee 7821:36
    Tempo: the single “Neuschnee” played back at 78 rpm. Same song, pitched up until it sounds frantic — the first proof that side two is a trick.
  6. 6. Super 1624:09
    Tempo: “Super” slowed to 16 rpm. The same riff dragged down until it growls. Play it against “Super” at the end and hear one song become two.
  7. 7. Neuschnee27:48
    Form: the straight version of the single, finally. A clean, driving pop song that shows what side two has been cutting up.
  8. 8. Cassetto31:56
    Texture: the song played through a cassette recorder — muffled, wobbling, distant. The medium becomes the instrument.
  9. 9. Super 7833:45
    Tempo: “Super” at 78 rpm. A minute and a half of chipmunk urgency. The joke lands because the riff survives the speed.
  10. 10. Hallo Excentrico35:21
    Rhythm: the longest of the manipulations. The beat stretches and stutters as the tape drags — listen for where the loop slips out of time.
  11. 11. Super39:06
    Form: the original single closes the record. After ten tracks of versions, the source finally arrives last.

ADVENTUROUS
CAN 1972

Ege Bamyasi

You bounced off Can in week 1. Fair — that was a seventy-minute double album. This is the second hearing: forty minutes, seven songs, and a groove that never once breaks.

Verdict: the adventurous one — Can at their most focused, and the record that makes the case for the band in under forty minutes.

Scores: Pitchfork 9.8/10 (the indie review publication; near-perfect). Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums, 2020 edition: No. 454. Pitchfork’s No. 19 album of the 1970s.

The moment: recorded between December 1971 and June 1972 at Inner Space, a converted cinema in Weilerswist that the band soundproofed with 1,500 seagrass mattresses bought from army barracks. “Spoon” — the theme to the German TV thriller Das Messer — reached No. 6 in Germany and sold 300,000 copies, and the money paid for the studio. United Artists set a hard June deadline; a day before it, only three tracks were done. “Soup” was a ten-minute improvisation recorded that afternoon and kept, whatever the outcome.

Lineage: the tight, funk-driven side of krautrock — avant-funk, as the critics call it. Pitchfork called “Vitamin C” the best funk ever to come out of Europe. The record helped popularize krautrock and fed into Brian Eno, Sonic Youth, and Pavement.

Then vs now: Melody Maker called Can “without doubt the most talented and most consistent experimental rock band in Europe” on release; the retrospective consensus calls this their most focused record. The bounce-off was Tago Mago. This one is half the length and twice the focus.

Why you: the rematch. Same band, same musicians, a deadline forcing them to stop sprawling — and the context (a TV hit paying for a cinema studio) is the way in.

LISTEN FOR — Listen for the groove that never breaks. Jaki Liebezeit’s drums hold a steady pulse while Damo Suzuki’s voice floats above it — “Vitamin C” is the whole lesson in three and a half minutes.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Pinch0:00
    Rhythm: Liebezeit’s drums set a tight, dry pattern and the band plays around it without ever leaving it. Nine minutes that never loosen their grip.
  2. 2. Sing Swan Song9:28
    Dynamics: the record’s quietest moment — a lullaby after the tension of “Pinch.” Karoli’s guitar sounds like it is being played in another room.
  3. 3. One More Night14:17
    Form: the most compact song on the record. Dry arrangement, nothing wasted — the verse arrives, does its work, leaves.
  4. 4. Vitamin C19:53
    Rhythm: the drums lock into a marching pattern and two guitar chords answer. Three and a half minutes, and the best funk ever to come out of Europe.
  5. 5. Soup23:27
    Form: ten minutes of improvisation, recorded in one afternoon against a deadline. The band stretches out and the track wanders — then snaps back to the groove.
  6. 6. I’m So Green33:58
    Rhythm: a James Brown-style backbeat under a throwaway pop tune. The lightest track on the record, and the closest to a single.
  7. 7. Spoon37:03
    Texture: a drum machine ticks under live drums — Can’s first use of one — while the melody floats. The TV-theme hit that paid for the studio.

ADVENTUROUS
TANGERINE DREAM 1974

Phaedra

Seventeen minutes of one pattern cycling, shifting, and refusing to resolve — and it reached No. 15 in Britain on word of mouth alone, with virtually no airplay.

Verdict: the adventurous one — the week’s purest sequencer record: no songs, no vocals, just machines learning to breathe.

Scores: AllMusic 5/5 (the reference guide’s full marks). Listed in 1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die (the canon reference book). No. 38 on Q and Mojo’s 40 Cosmic Rock Albums.

The moment: recorded in November and December 1973 at The Manor in Oxfordshire — Tangerine Dream’s first album for Virgin, after Richard Branson signed them on the strength of Berlin tapes and they bought a modular Moog with the advance. It was their first album using the Moog sequencer, and tuning took hours a day — no presets, no memory. By day 11 they had six minutes on tape; the tape machine broke, the console failed repeatedly, the bass damaged the speakers. “Mysterious Semblance” was cut in one take on a Mellotron — a keyboard that plays back taped sounds — with Froese’s wife turning the knobs while he played.

Lineage: the Berlin School — sequencer-driven electronic music that trades songs for slowly shifting patterns. The title track’s oscillators drifted out of tune as the analog gear warmed up, and the drift is in the recording.

Then vs now: no airplay, a 15-week run to No. 15 in the UK, a gold disc in seven countries — and barely 6,000 copies sold in Germany. Now it is the band’s defining record and the template for the ambient and Berlin School music that followed.

Why you: the technique, uncut. If you want to hear what a sequencer does when nobody is singing over it, the whole lesson is in the title track.

LISTEN FOR — Listen for the sequencer pattern that opens the title track — one cycling figure, repeating and shifting, and the whole seventeen minutes grows out of it.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Phaedra0:00
    Texture: a sequencer pattern starts cycling and layers pile on — Mellotron, synth, bass notes that drift out of tune as the gear warms. Seventeen minutes built from one figure.
  2. 2. Mysterious Semblance At The Strand Of Nightmares11:10
    Timbre: a Mellotron solo treated with slowly sweeping filters. Recorded in one take, and the single instrument fills the whole room.
  3. 3. Movements Of A Visionary21:04
    Rhythm: the sequencer takes over as bass — pulsing notes where a bass guitar would sit. The pattern shifts, and the track follows it.
  4. 4. Sequent C29:02
    Timbre: a recorder with tape echo. Two minutes of quiet after the machines — the shortest track, and the only acoustic sound on the record.
  5. 5. Delfi32:08
    Form: the closing track gathers the sequencer patterns back up and lets them run. The album ends mid-motion, not with a resolution.

WILD CARD
SUICIDE 1977

Suicide

Two men, one organ, and a voice that sounds like Elvis dragged through a New York alley. The wild card converts on story, not specs — and this one has the strongest story of the week.

Verdict: the wild card — punk without guitars, pop without choruses, and the record that scared America so badly Rolling Stone called it “absolutely puerile.”

Scores: Pitchfork 9.1/10 (the indie review publication). AllMusic 4.5/5 (the reference guide). Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums, 2020 edition: No. 498; Pitchfork’s No. 39 album of the 1970s. Robert Christgau gave it a C+ at the time — the contemporary pan is part of the story.

The moment: recorded in four days at Ultima Sound in Blauvelt, New York, and released in December 1977 — the songs were already written from five years of live shows. Suicide was the first act signed to Marty Thau’s Red Star label. “Frankie Teardrop,” ten and a half minutes, came from a newspaper story Alan Vega read about a factory worker who lost his job and murdered his wife and child; Vega improvised the lyrics trying to inhabit both the worker and his family.

Lineage: the blueprint for post-punk, synth-pop, and industrial rock. Bruce Springsteen said “State Trooper” was heavily influenced by “Frankie Teardrop”; M.I.A. sampled “Ghost Rider” for “Born Free.”

Then vs now: the UK press liked it; America panned it and it failed to chart anywhere. Within a few years it was regarded as a milestone in electronic and rock music — the record nobody bought became the one everybody cites.

Why you: the wild card is the test of the week’s concept — context as appreciation. Knowing the story doesn’t make it easier. It makes it land.

LISTEN FOR — Listen for what’s missing. No guitar, no bass — just an organ drone, a pulsing rhythm, and a voice. “Frankie Teardrop” tells a ten-minute story over almost nothing, and the nothing is the point.
TRACK NOTES
  1. 1. Ghost Rider0:00
    Rhythm: a pulsing electronic beat under a two-note organ riff. Vega’s voice rides on top like a rockabilly singer who wandered into the wrong room.
  2. 2. Rocket USA2:33
    Voice: Vega chants over a grinding organ drone. The track barely changes for four minutes, and the repetition is the menace.
  3. 3. Cheree6:50
    Melody: the closest thing to a love song here — a simple, almost pretty melody over the same cheap organ. The tenderness makes the rest of the record hit harder.
  4. 4. Johnny10:32
    Voice: Vega leans into ’50s rock-and-roll phrasing. The melody could be a doo-wop song if the organ underneath weren’t so cold.
  5. 5. Girl12:43
    Dynamics: the quietest track — a slow, spare ballad. The organ thins to almost nothing and Vega’s voice carries it alone.
  6. 6. Frankie Teardrop16:49
    Form: ten minutes, one story, almost no chord changes. The tension comes from the voice getting more desperate while the music refuses to move.
  7. 7. Che27:15
    Texture: the album closes with a drone that sounds like a machine left running. Five minutes of hum and echo after the story ends.
